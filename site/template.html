Introduction

What metadynamics is for

  1. The problem: rare events

    Molecular dynamics follows atoms femtosecond by femtosecond, but many processes we care about take microseconds to hours: a ligand leaving its pocket, a protein changing shape, an ion pair separating in water, a crystal starting to grow. The system spends nearly all its time rattling inside a free-energy basin. The waiting time to escape grows exponentially with the barrier height, so every extra kBT of barrier multiplies the wait by e ≈ 2.7.

  2. Describe the process with a few coordinates

    Pick a small number of collective variables s(R), such as a distance, an angle or a coordination number, that tell the states apart. The free energy along s comes from how often each value would be seen at equilibrium. Its minima are the stable states and its saddles are the transition states.

  3. The idea: fill the wells you have already visited

    Metadynamics adds a bias that depends on the history of the run [1, 3]. Every τG it drops a small Gaussian hill at the current value of s, which discourages the system from returning there. The hills pile up in the basin until it overflows, the system escapes over the lowest saddle, and the next basin fills. Once the whole landscape is filled the system diffuses freely, and the accumulated bias is the free energy turned upside down [3, 6].

  4. Well-tempered: shrink the hills as you go

    With hills of fixed size the estimate never stops fluctuating. Well-tempered metadynamics makes each new hill smaller where the bias is already high, so the bias settles in a single run [5, 3]. It converges to a known fraction of −F, and the collective variable is sampled as if at the higher temperature T + ΔT while everything else stays at T.

  5. From free energies to rates

    If hills are added slowly enough that the transition state never receives any bias, the biased run still crosses the barrier the way the real system does, only sooner. Each simulated step then counts for eβV steps of real time [9, 4]. Repeating the run many times gives escape times that should follow an exponential distribution, and a statistical test checks that they do [10, 4].

  6. What can go wrong

    Everything rests on the choice of collective variables. If a slow motion is missing from s, the bias fills a well that the system cannot actually leave along s, and both the free energy and the rates come out wrong. Hill height, width and spacing control the trade-off between speed and accuracy. The demonstrations below show each of these effects on models small enough to run in about a minute.

Tests A and B are two-coordinate toy models with overdamped Langevin dynamics at 300 K (kBT = 0.596 kcal/mol), built to match the shape and key energies of the paper’s Figs. 2 and 3. They are not the real NaCl or dipeptide systems. Model A: hills W = 0.3 kcal/mol with widths 0.12 Å and 4 units of v. Model B: hills 0.2 kcal/mol, width 14°. In demos 1–3, all curves come from demos.py (seed 7) re-run with every hill, trajectory point and walker recorded; nothing is smoothed or re-simulated in the browser.
